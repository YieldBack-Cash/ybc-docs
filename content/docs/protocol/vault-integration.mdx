Every market sits on top of a vault, and the vault's role is to abstract an underlying protocol.
This page is about that integration.

## The four calls

| Function | Called by | For |
|----------|-----------|-----|
| `query_asset()` | Router, Yield Manager | The underlying asset's address |
| `convert_to_assets(shares)` | Yield Manager | Assets per share — the raw observation behind the exchange rate |
| `deposit(assets, receiver, from, operator)` | Router, Yield Manager | Asset → shares |
| `redeem(shares, receiver, owner, operator)` | Router, Yield Manager | Shares → assets |

That is the entire surface. SEP-56 declares eighteen functions; YBC calls four of
them and nothing vault-specific, which is what lets any compliant vault back a
market without a per-vault adapter.

## Resolving the asset: once per invocation

`query_asset` is read live. It is resolved exactly
**once per invocation** and threaded through every leg.

## Reading the rate

Only the yield manager ever calls `convert_to_assets`. The AMM prices its
reserve through the yield manager's `get_exchange_rate`. The yield manager contract's exchange rate
is the only rate honored throughout the protocol.

PT settles at the yield manager's rate, not the vault's. The two agree while a
vault only appreciates and separate the moment it loses value, since the yield
manager [high-water-marks](/concepts/exchange-rate) while a raw vault read
follows the value down.

This also closes off the flash callbacks' `vault_rate` argument — now a
misnomer, since the pool can only pass back the yield manager's own rate. The
design previously relied on the pool passing a *raw* vault reading, a
precondition that could be documented but not enforced.

The pairing holds by construction: `create_market` threads one `vault` address
into both contracts and `set_pool` is one-shot.


## Reading the rate: once per invocation

The rate is loaded once at the top of an invocation and reused for every
conversion within it. A swap needs it at four points: reserve pricing before,
the traded amount, the fee cut, reserve pricing after. All four read the
same cached value.

This is safe because the rate cannot move mid-invocation, so caching changes no
result. It matters because `convert_to_assets` is not a cheap getter: a vault
that lends its deposits may accrue interest and read its underlying reserves to
answer, and that cost lands inside the caller's transaction budget.

<Callout type="info">
**Related material.** On Soroban the number of cross-contract calls is itself a
scarce resource. See [Improve memory metering for WASM VMs](https://github.com/orgs/stellar/discussions/1970).
</Callout>

Past maturity the rate is locked, so it answers from the yield manager's storage
without touching the vault at all.

## Custody: the yield manager holds the collateral

the **yield manager** custodies every vault share, so when a user redeems it is both the `owner` and the `operator`
with the user as the `receiver`.

The **router** holds nothing, including during [zaps](/using-ybc/zaps). Every leg
names the *user* as the token-holding party: a zap deposit passes the user as
`from`, `receiver` and `operator` alike. A failure anywhere reverts the whole transaction.

## Signed amounts versus measured amounts

This constraint shapes the whole zap API. A user signs their authorization
*before* execution, so any figure inside a signature must be one they chose. But
share counts are only knowable at execution time.

The protocol splits the two:

- **Caller-chosen amounts are signable** — `assets` in, `min_asset_out`, the
  allowance ceilings and their expiries.
- **Measured amounts never appear in a signature.** Share counts are computed
  mid-call and consumed under a ceiling the user signed in advance.

Two mechanisms fall out of that, and they differ:

| | Ordinary zaps | `exit_expired_to_asset` |
|---|---|---|
| What gets redeemed | Only the shares **this call produced**, freshly measured | The user's **whole** share balance, up to the ceiling |
| Role of `sweep_allowance` | An asserted ceiling — the call fails if the zap produced more | The actual figure taken |
| Who drives `redeem` | The router, as SEP-56 **operator**, consuming the allowance | The yield manager, which authenticates `from` |

<Callout type="warn">
Size `sweep_allowance` to the expected proceeds rather than passing something
arbitrarily large. For `exit_expired_to_asset` it is a ceiling on your entire
vault-share balance, **including shares held for unrelated markets**.

Do not derive `sweep_expiry` from the current ledger — that reintroduces exactly
the simulation/execution drift this design exists to eliminate.
</Callout>

In both cases the proceeds go straight to the user. The router's authority is
only to *trigger* the conversion, never to redirect it.
