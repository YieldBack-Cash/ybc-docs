Every market sits on top of a vault, and the whole protocol touches it through
**four functions**. This page is about that boundary: who calls what, when, and
what the protocol refuses to take the vault's word for.

## The four calls

| Function | Called by | For |
|----------|-----------|-----|
| `query_asset()` | Router, Yield Manager | The underlying asset's address |
| `convert_to_assets(shares)` | Yield Manager **only** | Assets per share — the raw observation behind the exchange rate |
| `deposit(assets, receiver, from, operator)` | Router, Yield Manager | Asset → shares |
| `redeem(shares, receiver, owner, operator)` | Router, Yield Manager | Shares → assets |

That is the entire surface. SEP-56 declares eighteen functions; YBC calls four of
them and nothing vault-specific, which is what lets any compliant vault back a
market without a per-vault adapter.

## Resolving the asset: once per invocation

`query_asset` is read **live** on every call rather than snapshotted into the
factory's market record — there is nothing to migrate and nothing that can go
stale.

But it is resolved exactly **once per invocation** and threaded through every
leg, and that part is a security property rather than an optimisation:

<Callout type="warn">
A vault that named one asset on the way in and a different one on the way out
could otherwise be paid in the valuable token and settle in a worthless one.
</Callout>

## Reading the rate: the AMM asks the yield manager, not the vault

This is the part most worth understanding, because the obvious design is the
wrong one.

**Only the yield manager ever calls `convert_to_assets`.** The AMM prices its
vault-share reserve through the **yield manager's** `get_exchange_rate`, not
through a direct vault read.

The reason is that PT is a claim on `face / rate` shares where `rate` is the
*yield manager's* rate — because that is what `redeem_principal` actually settles
at. The vault's own rate never enters PT's payout.

The two numbers are identical for as long as a vault only appreciates, which is
what hid the difference. **They separate the moment a vault loses value:** the
yield manager [high-water-marks](/concepts/exchange-rate) while a direct vault
read follows the value down. A pool reading the vault directly would then price
PT face at the depressed rate, while the yield manager would only ever redeem it
at the high-water mark — pricing PT above what it can pay out, for anyone holding
it, in unlimited size for anyone willing to mint it.

Reading the yield manager makes the pool price PT against the number that will
actually settle it, in every vault regime.

### What this does to the flash-swap rate hint

The `vault_rate` argument on the flash callbacks is now a slight misnomer: the
figure the pool passes is the **yield manager's own rate making a round trip**.
The pool obtained it from `get_exchange_rate` to price the trade, which already
committed the ratchet, so re-applying it in the yield manager is idempotent and
the non-decreasing floor is a no-op.

That is a *stronger* guarantee than the one the design used to rest on. Formerly
the pool passed a raw vault reading and was trusted never to pass an
already-high-water-marked one — a precondition that could only be documented, not
enforced, and whose failure direction (a value too **high**) would have ratcheted
the stored rate up permanently. The pool can no longer supply any value except
the one already in storage.

The pairing is guaranteed by construction rather than checked: `create_market`
threads one `vault` address into both the yield manager and the pool's
share-token slot, and `set_pool` is one-shot, so the two can never be re-pointed.

## Reading the rate: once per invocation, and what it costs

Against a lending vault a rate *read* is not cheap — Blend's `convert_to_assets`
accrues interest and reads the underlying pool's reserve to answer.

A swap needs the rate at four points: reserve pricing before the trade, the
traded amount, the fee cut, and reserve pricing after. Making four calls is what
pushed the flash-swap zaps past the per-transaction budget on testnet. The pool
now loads it **once per invocation** and reuses it — the rate cannot move
mid-invocation, so caching changes no result.

Routing through the yield manager costs one extra contract frame:

```text
vault.convert_to_assets    8,124 stroops   (what the pool used to call)
ym.get_exchange_rate      12,564 stroops   (what it calls now)
```

About 4,440 stroops per swap for the frame plus the yield manager's instance
write — against a live lending vault the rate ratchets on nearly every ledger, so
that write is the steady-state case rather than the exception. The underlying
vault is still read exactly once per invocation, just through the yield manager.

Two side effects worth knowing:

- **Past maturity it is strictly cheaper.** The yield manager's rate is locked, so
  it answers from its own storage without touching the vault at all.
- **It keeps the yield manager's instance alive.** An expired yield-manager
  instance bricks the whole market, so that TTL bump is wanted anyway.

## Custody: the yield manager holds, the router never does

The **yield manager** custodies every deposited vault share. When it redeems from
its own custody it is owner and operator alike, so the vault's authorization is
satisfied by invoker auth at execution time — nothing there ever enters a user's
signature.

The **router** holds nothing, including during [zaps](/using-ybc/zaps). Every leg
names the *user* as the token-holding party: a zap deposit passes the user as
`from`, `receiver` and `operator` alike. A revert anywhere unwinds the chain and
strands nothing.

## Amounts are measured, never taken on trust

SEP-56 leaves fees and rounding to the implementation, so **no quantity crossing
the vault boundary is trusted as reported**. Every one is measured as a balance
delta around the call:

```rust
let shares_before = vault_token.balance(to);
VaultContractClient::new(e, vault).deposit(&assets, to, to, to);
let shares_out = vault_token.balance(to) - shares_before;
assert!(shares_out > 0, "vault minted no shares");
```

The same pattern covers redemption, and the caller's `min_asset_out` /
`max_asset_in` bound is enforced against the measured figure. A vault that
under-delivers fails the bound rather than passing a flattering return value.

## Signed amounts versus measured amounts

This constraint shapes the whole zap API. A user signs their authorization
*before* execution, so any figure inside a signature must be one they chose. But
share counts are only knowable at execution time.

The protocol splits the two:

- **Caller-chosen amounts are signable** — `assets` in, `min_asset_out`, the
  allowance ceilings and their expiries.
- **Measured amounts never appear in a signature.** Share counts are computed
  mid-call and consumed under a ceiling the user signed in advance.

Two mechanisms fall out of that, and they differ:

| | Ordinary zaps | `exit_expired_to_asset` |
|---|---|---|
| What gets redeemed | Only the shares **this call produced**, freshly measured | The user's **whole** share balance, up to the ceiling |
| Role of `sweep_allowance` | An asserted ceiling — the call fails if the zap produced more | The actual figure taken |
| Who drives `redeem` | The router, as SEP-56 **operator**, consuming the allowance | The yield manager, which authenticates `from` |

<Callout type="warn">
Size `sweep_allowance` to the expected proceeds rather than passing something
arbitrarily large. For `exit_expired_to_asset` it is a ceiling on your entire
vault-share balance, **including shares held for unrelated markets**.

Do not derive `sweep_expiry` from the current ledger — that reintroduces exactly
the simulation/execution drift this design exists to eliminate.
</Callout>

In both cases the proceeds go straight to the user. The router's authority is
only to *trigger* the conversion, never to redirect it.

## What YBC deliberately never calls

SEP-56 also declares `mint`, `withdraw`, `convert_to_shares`, `total_supply`,
`total_assets`, four `max_*` and four `preview_*`. The subset is kept **exactly**
minimal — every declared method is called somewhere, and nothing called is
missing.

That exactness is deliberate. Declaring a method the protocol never invokes is
not free: **it reads as a requirement, and a vault that omits it looks
incompatible when it is not.** `convert_to_shares` was declared once for symmetry
with `convert_to_assets`; nothing called it, and blend-vault-v2 does not
implement it. It was removed.

Three exclusions are worth naming:

**`preview_*`** would be the natural way to quote a trade, but a vault's quote is
only advisory — settlement measures actual balance deltas and enforces the
caller's bound, which is strictly more trustworthy. Depending on them would also
pass against a fully compliant mock while failing against a production vault that
omits them.

<Callout type="info">
**Frontends should call `preview_*` directly for display.** Quoting is a UI
concern; the contracts deliberately do not do it.
</Callout>

**`max_*`** cannot be relied on to detect a vault with no liquidity right now —
the reference implementations just convert the owner's balance and say nothing
about availability.

**`withdraw`** is skipped in favour of the share-denominated `redeem`: "convert
exactly these shares" needs no preview round-trip and leaves no dust behind.

## Two things SEP-56 does not guarantee

The protocol depends on both, and neither is enforceable at this boundary:

1. **Share value may fall.** The yield manager assumes a non-decreasing rate.
2. **Fees are out of scope**, so `convert_to_assets` may overstate what `redeem`
   actually pays out.

A vault violating either leaves PT over-valued against backing that cannot cover
it — a slow solvency drift rather than an error, and nothing on-chain detects it.
Everything else a vault can get wrong fails loudly: the transaction reverts and
the user keeps their position.

What that means for choosing a market is covered under [Risks](/concepts/risks).
